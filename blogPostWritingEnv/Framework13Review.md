# Framework 13 Laptop 3-Month Review

> I purchased the DIY edition of a Framework 13, with the last-gen AMD Ryzen™ PRO 7040 processor, I configured it with 16 GB of DDR5 Ram, and a 2TB SSD

[Photo of my new laptop](image1)

### There are three main reasons I wanted to pick up a Framework 13 laptop:

The first is that my previous laptop, a 13 inch HP laptop of some variety was frankly a mess. Honestly I feel it is rude to complain, as the computer was given to me for free. But that did not change the issues and limitations it had: the processor was quite a few generations old, the screen had some kind of delamination issues, and to top it all off it did not have a battery! While the laptop still worked perfectly fine when plugged in, and was a significant improvement over my 2014 Macbook Pro in terms of computing capabilities, the thing was simply no good.

Secondly, I just wanted a fancy new laptop! All of my previous laptops were bought with budget in mind, and a few years out of date when they were bought by me. I've never had a truly new laptop experience, and the process of building and owning a Framework was touted to be quite unique as far as laptops go. I wanted to experience that!

 To tell you the final reason I wanted this laptop will first require me to put my tinfoil-hat on for a moment: As more corporations embrace AI, and with the ongoing consumer chip shortage, I am growing increasingly concerned that we are approaching a zenith of personal computing, The worry is that the companies building consumer-class chips will continue to shift production to server-grade silicone. The shift will be for both monetary gain, and an increase in control over the end-user; if independently capable computers are no longer available, consumers will be forced to purchase thin-clients tuned to talk to cloud based infrastructure, thus allowing hosting companies to add additional subscriptions fees on top of ones normal wifi bill, and will give more power to the cloud provider as they use their control to surveil, moderate, and advertise to the end user. I recognize that this is verymuch a worst-case scenario, but it is also something I am truly concerned about. During my time in College, the way computers meant to me changed, and the capabilities they had to have changed as well. Laptops have become the primary computer I use in the day-to-day, and as such the laptop I call mine should be personal, reliable, repairable, and capable. Framework as a company has committed themselves to making devices for end users first, and I respect that.

So with my reasons for wanting one out of the way, why would I buy from Framework? These laptops are *expensive* devices for the performance you are getting. This is due to several reasons: Framework cannot subsidize the cost of a computer with licensing deals between software companies like Microsoft, they don't come bundled with warranty plans or subscription upgrade paths, and building a repairable component is much more expensive than a conventional one, as if any given piece of plastic is meant to be removed, handled, then put back it has to be built stronger to handle the punishment, and to be designed differently so the user does not break the component due to the way in which it was attached to the rest of the device. All of these factors combine to make framework machines quite expensive as far as laptops go. I knew all of these things well before the purchase was made, and I am fine with them. This laptop is intended to last me a very long time, and to me, the large upfront cost coming along with repairability is beyond acceptable.

## Thoughts on the Physical Aspects of the Laptop:

Buying a laptop online is always a gamble as you are not able to see the size of the laptop before you have purchased it, I was honestly a little concerned about the form factor of the Framework 13, as the laptop is built with a rather strange 3:2 aspect ratio I was a little worried that it would be too wide when fitting into my backpack, and the screen being a strange size for applications. After using the laptop for several months, I am happy to report that this fear was unfounded! The system fits perfectly in my backpack, most modern apps and websites are built to scale with very tall screens, and the increased depth of the wrist wrest area actually makes the laptop very comfortable to use while on my lap in bed.

Unfortunately the build-quality of the bottom case of this laptop is not perfect. The screen and top lid is built well, all of the internal components are well designed, and the connectors are both ergonomic and well labeled. However the keyboard/ input cover has a noticeable seam with the bottom chassis, and the stamped aluminum of said bottom chassis feels rather cheap for the price paid. It's not bad by any means, and despite the issues this laptop’s build quality is still worlds better than my previous device. But I would be lying if I said I was not a bit disappointed by the build quality of the bottom half.

[Photo of the bottom half of my laptop](image2)

· I purchased my laptop several months before Framework launched the Pro variant of their 13” device. I'm not disappointed that I was unable to purchase a Pro model, as they are wildly out of my price range. But I am jealous of the milled aluminum bottom chassis, input cover, and haptic touchpad. I am absolutely planning on upgrading to the pro chassis as soon as the upgrade kits hit the market.

One of the most unique features of Framework computers are the slottable IO and expansion bays, with the 13 inch models like this one coming with 4. I know several folks in the Framework community carry several such cards with them, or otherwise have them on hand to change their laptops capabilities on the fly, but I mainly see this feature as a future-proofing measure, and a protection against the ports being damaged. I have my laptop configured with x2 USB-C ports, and x2 USB-A ports. I have an HDMI port card on hand when I'm at home, but I have not needed one for a long time, and don't expect to anytime soon. It may seem a bit silly to have that many USB ports on a single device, but I frequently transfer data to, and between my entertainment devices. I have had to use 3 USB ports at once on two separate occasions.

Thats all of the parts that I truly have thoughts on, so here a quick couple of bullet points/ attributes that really stood out to me:

- It does not have a trackpoint nipple (-_-
- Having clear purple bezels makes me perhaps a bit too happy
- It has adequate cooling, but can get quite loud
- The laptop is equipped with a fingerprint scanner in the power button. I do not use mine, instead I log into the system with a short pin
- I went with the Traditional Chinese keyboard, as I would like to learn the language one day. Unfortunately that keyboard has a windows super key, rather than a framework logo super key.
- The physical mute and camera shutter switches are nice touches, and I always have both closed.
- I really like the way Framework placed their logo on the laptop. It's similar to what Apple does, which is a good thing, as Apple knows how to make a sexy laptop.

## How it runs and how I use it

First and foremost, the Framework has a battery!!! This is a huge deal for me, as my previous laptop did not have one, requiring me to plug it into the wall anytime I wanted to use it. While this did not hamper the functionality of the machine in any way, It severely damaged the way in which it could be used, as anytime I wanted to move seats, or take it away from my desk to a cafe I would have to save and close all of my apps and work before doing so. Admittedly a minor inconvenience, and one I could have solved by purchasing a new battery for the laptop, a minor expense I was too proud to commit to as I never liked the old HP to start with. The Framework 13’s battery life is known to be quite lackluster, only getting about 4-6 hours, but this has honestly not been an issue for me, the two times I have run it down in battery I have also had access to a power outlet, and I carry an Apple 65W type-C charger in my backpack at all times.

My OS of choice is NixOs Linux with the Gnome desktop environment. I've tried a couple different desktop environments over the months, but I keep coming back to gnome after a while. It just has so many creature-comforts that other DE’s lack. Things like the screenshot function being able to record video, a surprisingly comprehensive settings menu, and a simple and appealing UI keep on bringing me back. Plus, the community of Gnome extensions make it easy to change the system, or fix any issues. Recently I have been quite happy with PaperWM as my window manager, a strongly-typed window manager tuned to scrolling between windows to the left and right.

I try to only use the laptop to work on creative pursuits. Things like writing blog posts, writing code for the small projects I have, and working on blender, or other CAD suites. I do not use it for work, and only consume entertainment in the form of videogames on the laptop. To that end, It's quite fast! Able to run up to dark souls, and just about all of the blender projects I have worked on so far Unfortunately the system really struggles trying to run Dark Souls 3, but aside from that one game I am quite happy with the performance available on this device. Besides, when the desire to play Elden ring, or a similarly graphically intensive game takes me, I have a much more capable desktop computer available.

## Closing thoughts

Overall, I am extremely satisfied with this laptop so far. For the way I use a computer this machine is the perfect size and form-factor, The performance is, while not exceptional very satisfactory for what I paid for this device and will be plenty power for what I need it to do, and I am extremely excited for the ability to upgrade, and repair the computer when the need to doso arises!
